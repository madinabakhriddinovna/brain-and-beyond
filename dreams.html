What your brain does when you dream

Move your cursor ✦

Every night, your brain becomes an artist, a storyteller and a quiet healer. And you rarely remember any of it.

Every night, your brain goes through sleep in cycles of about 90 minutes. At the end of each cycle comes a special stage: dream sleep, or REM sleep. You visit it four to six times a night, and each visit gets longer toward morning.

When it begins, a signal from your brainstem changes everything. Your eyes start moving quickly under your eyelids. Your brain becomes almost as active as when you're awake. The areas for vision and emotion light up, which is why dreams feel so vivid and so full of feeling.

But three things are very different.

The logical brain goes quiet

The front of your brain, the part that checks whether things make sense, slows down. So you can fly, meet someone who passed away, or switch cities in a second, and nothing feels strange.

Your body is switched off

Your brainstem switches off most of your muscles, so you don't act out your dreams. If you wake up while this is still happening, you can briefly be unable to move. That's sleep paralysis.

The stress chemical disappears

This is the most fascinating part. The brain's main stress chemical, noradrenaline, almost disappears during dream sleep. It may be the only time your brain revisits emotional memories without the stress attached.

Some scientists believe this is why sleep helps soften painful experiences, like a kind of overnight therapy.

Why do we forget dreams so fast?

The brain chemistry that helps save new memories is very low in this stage. Unless you wake up during a dream, it usually fades soon after you wake.

So every night, your brain becomes an artist, a storyteller and a quiet healer.

And you rarely remember any of it.
